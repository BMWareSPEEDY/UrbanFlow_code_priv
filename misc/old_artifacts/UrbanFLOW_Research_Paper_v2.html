UrbanFLOW: A Physics-Guided Graph Neural Network Surrogate for Real-Time Urban Flood Inundation Modeling
Anonymous IRIS National Fair 2026–27 Submission
Subject Category: Earth & Environmental Sciences
Abstract—Urban flash floods caused by severe convective cloudbursts inflict billions in infrastructure damage and risk human life. Standard hydrodynamic numerical solvers (EPA SWMM 5.2 solving 1D/2D Saint-Venant equations) deliver high fidelity but require 15 to 45 minutes per run, making real-time municipal early warning impossible, while radar nowcasts lack junction-level granularity. This research presents UrbanFLOW, an autonomous deep Graph Neural Network surrogate designed to predict street junction flood depths in sub-100 ms without runtime physics solvers. Built on a 6-layer Graph Isomorphism Network with Edge Features (HydroGINE-v5), the model integrates micro-topographic DEM features, directional gravity vectors, dynamic FiLM modulation, and a topological Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) router. Evaluated across 16 global catchments (76,316 physical nodes) under a 50 mm/hr reference and a 100 mm/hr stress regime, the architecture preserves hydraulic mass continuity across complex street topologies. The model achieved a global MAE of 2.44 cm, a catchment Nash-Sutcliffe Efficiency (NSE) of 0.9128, an actionable hazard F1-score of 90.8% (≥ 0.15 m), and 99.4% categorical hazard recall at its 480 highest-consequence nodes (74.2% depth agreement within ±15 cm). With an average end-to-end HTTP API latency of 85.5 ms (yielding an effective ~1,310× to 8,690× computational speedup over EPA SWMM 5.2) and a live-computed 38.5% spatial capture rate on 13 geotagged documented flood locations from the October 2024 Bengaluru monsoon, UrbanFLOW provides municipal disaster control centers with hyper-local, sub-second predictive foresight for automated flood barrier activation and emergency dewatering pump dispatch.

1. Introduction & Related Work

1.1 The Convective Cloudburst Crisis

Global climate change has intensified localized, high-intensity convective precipitation events—commonly designated as cloudbursts—characterized by rainfall rates exceeding 50 to 100 mm/hr over short spatial and temporal scales. Concurrently, accelerating urban expansion has replaced natural pervious soils with impervious asphalt and concrete surfaces, diminishing infiltration capacities, shortening basin response times, and magnifying peak surface runoff volumes by up to 400%.

Urban drainage systems are primarily designed using historical Intensity-Duration-Frequency (IDF) curves for return intervals of 5 to 10 years. Under sudden cloudburst regimes, surface runoff quickly overwhelms curb inlets and storm sewer pipe capacities, inducing pipe surcharge, manhole geysering, and overland street inundation. Saturated transport networks, sunken highway underpasses, and basement complexes become life-threatening hydraulic traps within minutes of storm onset. Municipal emergency dispatchers therefore require hyper-local (junction-scale), sub-second inundation forecasts to automate traffic diversions, lower motorized flood barrier gates, and mobilize dewatering pump trucks proactively.

1.2 The Computational Bottleneck of Numerical Solvers

Municipal flood management has historically depended on physically based 1D/2D hydrodynamic numerical engines, with the US EPA Storm Water Management Model (SWMM 5.2) serving as the international engineering benchmark. SWMM 5.2 solves the 1D/2D Saint-Venant equations governing conservation of mass and momentum alongside Manning's friction and Horton infiltration [1].

To maintain numerical stability, SWMM 5.2 must satisfy numerical stability constraints (the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy condition), requiring time steps under 0.5 s on short urban street segments ($L_e < 10$ m). Simulating a 60-minute cloudburst across a moderate catchment of 3,000 to 13,000 nodes requires 15 to 45 minutes of dedicated CPU computation. This renders traditional solvers incapable of providing real-time early warning during flash storm events.

1.3 Limitations of Prior Machine Learning Approaches

Data-driven deep learning surrogates have been explored to bypass numerical cost, but conventional architectures suffer from structural deficiencies:

  • Pixel-Grid CNNs (U-Net, ConvLSTM): Rasterize urban topographies into regular 2D grids, enforcing Euclidean spatial invariance. They cannot represent subterranean pipe connectivity, street curb channelization, or anisotropic conduit capacities.
  • Standard MLPs: Evaluate nodes independently without topological message passing, failing to model hydraulic head propagation and upstream catchment accumulation.
  • Zero-Inflation Collapse: During any storm, 85–95% of nodes remain dry. Standard MSE minimization causes gradient collapse, producing smoothed near-zero depths everywhere and suppressing high-hazard ponding.
  • Graph Pooling Failure: Techniques like SAGPool compress nodes to extract global features. However, dropping intermediate nodes severs physical flow paths, causing catastrophic model failure (demonstrated in Section 8).

1.4 Contributions

This work establishes UrbanFLOW, an autonomous physics-guided graph neural surrogate. The primary contributions are:

  1. Topological Graph Representation: Urban road centerlines and 10 m DEM micro-topography are formulated into directed graphs $G = (V, E)$ with 32 scale-invariant physical features per node and 6 edge attributes, explicitly excluding raw coordinates to guarantee zero-shot geographic transferability.
  2. HydroGINE-v5 Neural Operator: A 6-layer Graph Isomorphism Network with edge-conditioned message passing, gravitational slope gating, and dynamic rainfall volume scaling.
  3. Decoupled FiLM Dual-Head Mechanism: Flood prediction is decomposed into a Margin-Based Focal hazard classifier (≥ 0.15 m) and a Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) continuous depth regressor, resolving zero-inflation gradient suppression.
  4. Non-Destructive Topological MoE Routing: Dynamically transitions between inland basin and coastal mountain hydraulic regimes without destructive graph pooling.
  5. Multi-Scale Global Benchmark: Evaluated across 16 catchments (76,316 nodes), achieving 2.44 cm MAE, 0.9128 NSE, 85.5 ms API latency (~1,310× to 8,690× speedup over SWMM 5.2), and a live-computed 38.5% spatial capture on 13 geotagged documented flood locations (October 2024 Bengaluru monsoon).

2. Mathematical Background & Governing Equations

2.1 Kirpich's Time of Concentration

Used to estimate the delay from rainfall onset to peak hydraulic discharge at a junction:

$$T_c = 0.0195 \cdot L^{0.77} \cdot S^{-0.385}$$ (1)

where $L$ is the maximum hydraulic length along the contributing drainage pathway (m) and $S$ is the dimensionless hydraulic slope (m/m).

2.2 Parabolic Sag Index

A geometric sag index identifies concave roadway profiles where steep incoming slopes flatten into low-gradient intersections, flagging topological depressions and bottleneck junctions vulnerable to manhole overflow.

2.3 Directional Gravity Gate

Biases message passing along edges based on physical slope to prevent uphill flow propagation:

$$g_{\text{gate}, uv} = \sigma\!\left(1.0 - 5.0 \cdot \text{ReLU}(S_{0, uv})\right)$$ (2)

Downhill edges ($S_0 \le 0$) remain fully open ($g_{\text{gate}} \approx 1.0$), while uphill edges are attenuated toward zero, physically preventing the neural network from predicting water running up a hill.

Urban catchments are formalized as directed graphs $G = (V, E)$, where $V$ represents street intersections, culverts, and topographic sink points ($|V| = N$), and $E$ represents street segments and storm conduits ($|E| = M$).

Edge Feature Matrix ($\mathbf{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times 6}$): Each directed edge $e_{uv} = (u, v)$ encodes six features: segment length $L_{uv}$ (m); longitudinal slope $S_{0,uv} = (z_u - z_v)/L_{uv}$ (m/m); carrying capacity $C_{uv}$ from road hierarchy; the gravity gate $g_{\text{gate},uv}$ defined in Eq. (2); and the coordinate deltas $\Delta x_{uv}$ and $\Delta y_{uv}$.

3.2 HydroGINE-v5 Message Passing

To preserve topological expressiveness equivalent to the Weisfeiler-Lehman (1-WL) graph isomorphism test, UrbanFLOW implements an edge-conditioned Graph Isomorphism Network (GINE) augmented with gravity gating. For each node $v$ at layer $k \in \{1, \ldots, 6\}$, the representation $\mathbf{h}_v^{(k)}$ is updated by scaling the previous representation with a learnable constant and pooling messages from all neighbors, where each neighbor contributes its representation conditioned on the gated edge feature vector (edge features multiplied by the gravity gate of Eq. (2)) and passed through a ReLU nonlinearity. The pooled sum is then transformed by a per-layer MLP.

Each layer incorporates Layer Normalization and a multi-scale residual skip connection that adds a damped copy ($\times 0.3$) of the previous layer's representation.

Multi-scale representation is captured by concatenating the terminal, intermediate (layer 3), and raw input embeddings into a single 288-dimensional vector $\mathbf{h}_{\text{cat},v}$.

3.3 Decoupled FiLM Dual-Head Mechanism

UrbanFLOW explicitly decouples whether a node floods from how deep the water reaches, eliminating zero-inflation gradient suppression (Fig. 2).

Fig. 2. Distribution of reference SWMM 5.2 junction depths across the 16-catchment benchmark (76,316 nodes, 50 mm/hr). The dry-dominated, zero-inflated field motivates the decoupled classification/regression hazard heads.

Head 1 — Discrete Hazard Classifier: A classifier head maps the 288-dimensional representation to a single hazard logit, passed through a sigmoid to yield the flood probability $p_{\text{hazard},v}$.

FiLM Conditioning Generator: The hazard probability is mapped by an MLP into per-junction affine parameters $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_v$ and $\boldsymbol{\beta}_v$ (two 288-dimensional vectors). The projection layer is zero-initialized, so at initialization the feature flow remains an identity.

Head 2 — Continuous Depth Regressor: The FiLM parameters scale and shift the representation element-wise before a regression MLP outputs the raw depth prediction $\hat{y}_{\text{raw},v}$. When the classifier determines a node is dry ($p_{\text{hazard}} \to 0$), FiLM scales down the regressor features, preventing spurious depth bleeding into dry elevated roadways.

4. Loss Function & Training

4.1 Multi-Task Objective & Hazard Classification

Training used a multi-task objective balancing discrete hazard classification and continuous depth regression. The classification head was trained using Focal Loss ($\gamma = 2.0$) with soft margins to overcome the fact that over 80% of road nodes remain dry during storms.

4.2 Asymmetric Safety Loss Multiplier

Depth residuals were penalized using smooth Huber regression ($\delta = 0.10$) on log-transformed depths. In disaster management, a false sense of security is fatal: if an intersection carries 50 cm of floodwater and the model predicts 10 cm, vehicles enter and become submerged. To strictly prevent hazardous under-predictions, the optimization applies an Asymmetric Safety Loss Multiplier $A_i$:

$$A_i = \begin{cases} 2.0 & \text{if under-predicting dangerous flood depth} \\ 1.5 & \text{if over-predicting on dry roads} \\ 1.0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$ (3)

Critical flood hazards ($\ge 0.30$ m) received higher loss sample weighting during training to prioritize high-consequence municipal bottlenecks.

5. Inference Pipeline: Universal Physical Continuity Bounding

At inference time, raw neural predictions pass through a non-differentiable physical bounding stack executed in 2.1 ms. The pipeline consists of five sequential stages:

5.1 Topological Hydraulic Regime Classification

Each node is classified into one of seven hydraulic regimes based on its physical features: choked surcharge, deep sink, valley depression, convergent sag, ridge crest, free-drain slope, or steep ridge.

5.2 Dynamic Activation Threshold ($\tau$)

A sigmoid confidence gate scales raw predictions based on morphological regime. Each node is assigned a threshold $\tau$: 0.15 for deep sinks or valley depressions, 0.25 for choked surcharges or convergent sags, 0.75 for ridge crests, free-drain slopes, or steep ridges, and 0.35 for default urban junctions. The confidence multiplier is a sharp logistic function of the hazard probability centered at $\tau$, scaling the raw prediction before bounding.

5.3 Mass-Conservation Upper Bounding

Predictions are capped by regime-dependent mass bounds derived from local depression volume and conduit capacity deficit. Ridge crests and steep slopes are clamped to 0.01 m; deep sinks are bounded by $1.5 \times d_{\text{dep}} + 0.3$ m (clamped to [1.0, 3.0] m); choked surcharges are bounded at 3.0 m.

5.4 Dry Ridge Clamping & Noise Removal

Flat dry nodes (low sink depth, low depression, low hazard probability) and steep ridges are forced to 0.0 m. Predictions below 0.02 m are zeroed. All outputs are clipped to [0.0, 3.0] m.

5.5 Water Surface Elevation (WSE) Backwater Envelope

Backwater ponding is constrained by the gravitational potential of upstream neighbors. For every node, the water surface elevation is its invert elevation plus its predicted depth. Each downstream node then receives, from each upstream neighbor, a backwater head equal to that neighbor's water surface elevation minus its own invert elevation (clamped above zero). The final bounded depth is the minimum of the confidence-scaled prediction and the greater of the maximum upstream backwater head and the local depression depth. This ensures water cannot pond higher than contributing adjacent hydraulic heads, eliminating physically impossible uphill water bleeding.

5.6 Production Weighted Ensemble

The final production model combines 4 specialized checkpoints using weighted averaging to smooth out variance before passing predictions through physical bounding rails:

$$\bar{y}_{\text{depth}} = \sum_{k=1}^{4} w_k \hat{y}_k$$ (4)

The ensemble weights are assigned as: $w_1 = 0.64$ (v5.10), $w_2 = 0.24$ (v5.11), $w_3 = 0.04$ (v5.0), and $w_4 = 0.08$ (Bangalore-optimized checkpoint).

6. Experimental Setup

6.1 The 16-Catchment Global Benchmark

The benchmark dataset spans 16 metropolitan basins across four continents, encompassing 76,316 physical street nodes representing diverse topographic typologies:

  1. Inland Plateaus & Tech Corridors (India): HSR Layout (1,379 nodes), Bellandur & ORR (1,507), Whitefield (1,797), Electronic City (3,337), Koramangala (4,416).
  2. Coastal Megacities & Island Harbors: Tokyo (13,173), Hong Kong (3,848), Singapore (2,777), Mumbai (2,741), New York City (3,828).
  3. Historic Riverine Floodplains: London Thames (10,528), Paris Seine (6,707), Berlin Spree (3,724), Delhi Yamuna (2,951).
  4. Lowland Deltas & Waterfront Basins: Bangkok Chao Phraya (10,399), Chicago Waterfront (3,204).

OpenStreetMap vector centerlines were extracted via Overpass API into directed graphs, projected to local UTM systems, and merged with 10 m SRTM/Copernicus DEM elevations.

6.2 Ground Truth Generation

Ground-truth inundation targets were generated using EPA SWMM 5.2 under full 1D/2D Dynamic Wave routing. Junctions were assigned $500$ m² surcharge areas, street centerlines parameterized as trapezoidal channels (Manning $n = 0.013$–$0.018$), and Horton infiltration applied ($f_0 = 75$ mm/hr, $f_\infty = 7.5$ mm/hr, $k = 4.0$ hr−1). Two precipitation stress regimes were used:

  • Design Cloudburst (50 mm/hr): Standard 10-year return storm, flooding 14,543 nodes globally.
  • Extreme Stress Test (100 mm/hr): Extreme convective storm, flooding 22,170 nodes globally.

Assembling the benchmark database required 68.4 hours of cumulative CPU execution.

8. Scientific Ablation & The SAGPool Failure Case

8.1 The SAGPool Topological Collapse

During architectural exploration, Self-Attention Graph Pooling (SAGPool) was evaluated to compress large graphs into multi-scale representations. SAGPool scores node significance and retains only the top $\lceil k \cdot N \rceil$ nodes. In steep coastal topographies such as Hong Kong (mean relief $\Delta z = 184$ m), pruning intermediate nodes severed the spatial continuity of drainage channels.

This motivated non-destructive, full-resolution topological message passing. Replacing graph pooling with the Topological MoE router preserves the full junction graph, enabling the production model to achieve a Hong Kong F1-score of 0.942 and a global MAE of 2.44 cm (Table 2).

8.2 Critical Failure Cases & Training Dynamics

Global Match Hinge Instability: Directly penalizing predictions outside ±12 cm via linear hinge loss caused over-prediction to expand as the linear gradient pulled dry cells into shallow puddle depths. Hydraulic stability was achieved through asymmetric Huber penalties and physical bounding rails.

Checkpoint Selection Criterion: Checkpoints selected purely on unconstrained raw MSE exhibited suboptimal live convergence on extreme topography, whereas evaluation incorporating post-rail physical bounding achieved >84% live match rate across complex basins.

10. System Architecture & Deployment

10.1 Backend Microservice

The serving backend is an asynchronous REST microservice using Flask/Gunicorn with pre-computed graph structures held in memory. The inference pipeline executes four stages:

  1. Dynamic Feature Modulation (1.2 ms): Rescales meteorological features for the given storm.
  2. HydroGINE-v5 Forward Pass (4.0–15.0 ms): Edge-conditioned message passing across 6 GINE layers.
  3. Physical Continuity Bounding (2.1 ms): Confidence gating, mass bounding, WSE backwater relaxation.
  4. GeoJSON Serialization (15–45 ms): Output formatting and HTTP delivery.

10.2 Municipal Decision Support

The frontend WebGL digital twin dashboard interfaces with municipal control centers through three automated decision-support modules (Fig. 5):

  • Automated Barrier Gate Actuation: Triggers motorized gate closure at underpass nodes predicted to exceed 0.30 m.
  • Mobile Dewatering Pump Dispatch: Optimizes spatial placement of trailer-mounted pumps based on hydraulic accumulation gradients.
  • Emergency Vehicle Dynamic Routing: Exports real-time travel-time penalty matrices to reroute ambulances and fire trucks around submerged intersections.

11. Limitations & Future Work

  1. Sub-Grid Micro-Topography: The model operates on 10 m resolution DEM. Features smaller than 10 m—15 cm highway curbs, retaining walls, flyover ramps—are smoothed during raster gridding. Sub-meter LiDAR datasets will be needed for centimeter-scale curb overflow modeling.
  2. Subsurface Pipe Clogging: UrbanFLOW assumes stormwater conduits operate at design capacity. Real-world debris accumulation, sediment siltation, and pipe collapse are not modeled and would require live sewer sensor feeds.
  3. Pluvial vs. Fluvial & Coastal Surge: The model is primarily a pluvial (surface runoff) surrogate. Coastal zones subject to storm surges or tidal stages require dynamic marine boundary conditions in future versions.

Future work will integrate live Doppler radar reflectivity feeds and edge IoT manhole water-level sensors for real-time model updating.

12. Conclusion

Urban flash flooding demands a shift from slow retrospective simulation to sub-second predictive foresight. This paper presented UrbanFLOW, an autonomous Graph Neural Network surrogate capable of predicting street junction flood depths in sub-100 millisecond latencies across entire metropolitan basins.

By combining the HydroGINE-v5 edge-conditioned message-passing backbone, a decoupled FiLM dual-head mechanism, and a non-destructive Topological MoE router, UrbanFLOW overcomes the dual challenges of numerical computational bottlenecks and zero-inflation gradient collapse. Benchmarked across 16 global catchments encompassing 76,316 nodes, the model achieved:

  • Global MAE of 2.44 cm (objective: < 5.0 cm)
  • Catchment NSE of 0.9128 (target: ≥ 0.80)
  • 99.4% hazard recall at the 480 highest-consequence nodes (74.2% depth agreement within ±15 cm)
  • Mass continuity error of 8.61% (< 10% standard)
  • API latency of 85.5 ms average (~1,310× to 8,690× speedup over SWMM 5.2)
  • Live-computed capture of 5/13 (38.5%) documented October 2024 Bengaluru flood locations

UrbanFLOW demonstrates that physics-guided graph neural operators can achieve numerical simulation parity at speeds enabling autonomous municipal barrier gate deployment and dewatering pump dispatch. This provides a scalable foundation for climate-resilient urban disaster early warning worldwide.
